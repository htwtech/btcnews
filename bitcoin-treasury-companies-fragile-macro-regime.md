194 public companies now hold Bitcoin. At first glance, that looks like broad institutional participation. But 4 of the 5 largest holders are Strategic-category firms, and together they control ~80% of all corporate BTC. So how "broad" is this wave, really?

## What Actually Happened in 2025

At the end of 2024, 22 public companies held more than 1,000 BTC. By the end of 2025, that number had doubled to 49. Together they now hold close to 5% of all Bitcoin in circulation — roughly $80 billion. One company dominates the picture. Strategy alone accounts for 738,731 BTC, more than 64% of the total.

Predictably, the narrative turned bullish. More companies should mean more demand. More structural support. And on the surface, that's not wrong — corporate treasuries do create a more stable holder base than retail-driven cycles. But 2026 is already showing that the picture underneath that headline is considerably more complicated.

## The Break: Adoption and Upside Are Not the Same Thing

The top 5 companies hold roughly 78% of all public corporate BTC. The companies outside the top 100 collectively hold just 2,622 BTC — essentially a rounding error in terms of market impact. So when we talk about "broad institutional uptake," we should be precise about what that means.

![Corporate Bitcoin holders by cohort](https://bvkmqlnhnryztqknjjah.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/content/news/Corporate%20Bitcoin%20holders%20by%20cohort.png)
<sub>Corporate Bitcoin holders by cohort. Source: Fidelity Digital Assets Research via Public Company Filings & Announcements, 12/15/25.</sub>

In their 2026 Look Ahead "Structural Shifts Beyond Price" Fidelity broke these 49 companies into three cohorts: Native (miners who accumulated through operations), Strategic (firms whose explicit purpose is holding BTC as a core treasury strategy), and Traditional (everyone else with a smaller allocation). The Strategic group, despite being only 12 of the 49, held ~80% of the total, with an average of ~12,350 BTC per company, well ahead of miners (~7,935 BTC) and traditional corporates (~4,326 BTC).

What that tells us is that 2025's expansion was driven by Bitcoin-maximalist treasury vehicles, not by a wave of mainstream corporations quietly diversifying their cash. The count of companies grew. The actual demand base stayed narrow.

## The Macro Regime Has Changed

We need to be honest about the environment, because it changes the calculus for every cohort differently. Inflation remains above the Fed's 2% target, with February CPI running at 2.4% y/y. Dollar strength creates drag on global liquidity. The October 2025 liquidation event left leverage residue, and re-leveraging has been slow since then. Fidelity's bull case for 2026 rests on QT ending, M2 expansion resuming, and a rotation out of the $7.5 trillion sitting in U.S. money market funds — plausible tailwinds, historically well-supported by BTC's M2 correlation, but not triggered in a sustained way yet.

The structural bid from corporate treasuries creates a floor. It doesn't by itself create an impulse upward.

## Not All Corporate Bitcoin Is the Same

When we break down corporate BTC holdings, the uniformity of the "adoption" narrative starts to fray further. The mNAV metric — market cap divided by Bitcoin NAV — shows huge dispersion across the cohort:

- Coinbase trades at ~50.3x its Bitcoin NAV, while Block trades at ~65.1x — because the market values their underlying businesses, not their BTC stash
- Strategy sits near ~0.98x NAV, priced as close to a pure BTC proxy as possible
- Some smaller Strategic companies trade at 0.13x NAV, meaning the market is actively discounting their Bitcoin holdings

The market already treats these cases differently, and has been for a while. Owning Bitcoin on a balance sheet provides structural support, but it doesn't create uniform demand for the equity — and the story of the underlying business still dominates.

There's also an encumbrance problem that doesn't show up in headline numbers. Take MARA: they report ~53,800 BTC on the balance sheet, but roughly 28% is loaned out or pledged as collateral — only ~72% is freely available. This kind of detail matters when markets get stressed, because encumbered reserves can't absorb liquidity shocks the way free holdings can. And honestly, this is probably underreported across the sector.

Funding method complicates the picture further. Companies that issued shares or convertible debt to buy Bitcoin have created dilution pressure on equity holders. When a treasury company sells new stock to fund BTC purchases, the net effect on early shareholders is more complicated than the "more Bitcoin = better" framing suggests.

## Why More Adoption Doesn't Equal Automatic Upside

Several dynamics are working against the simple "more buyers = higher prices" assumption:

1. **Concentration risk** — the initial wave from 22 to 49 large holders is largely priced in already. The next increment requires either large traditional corporations entering meaningfully, or current holders continuing to accumulate through harder conditions. Neither is a given.

2. **Miners becoming active managers** — the AI infrastructure boom is pulling mining companies away from pure accumulation. Cipher Mining signed a $5.5B AWS deal; IREN announced a $9.7B Microsoft cloud contract. At current economics, AI hosting is more profitable than Bitcoin mining for companies running ~20-25 joule equipment, which means some miners are now treating BTC as liquid capital rather than permanent reserves.

3. **Opposing flows** — some of the same "institutional adoption" buying Bitcoin on one side generates selling pressure on the other. Miners raising cash through BTC sales, treasury companies diluting equity to fund purchases — net demand is harder to calculate than adoption numbers imply.

The Ethereum parallel from 2025 is worth noting here. Dencun boosted network usage across the board, but worked partly by slashing fees — effectively giving away ~$100M in protocol revenue to accelerate adoption, while price underperformed through the whole period. Adoption metrics and asset economics can diverge for extended periods. The mechanism is different, but the pattern rhymes.

## Back to the Main Point

So, back to the question from the top: how broad is this wave, really? We think the honest answer is that it's structurally meaningful but operationally narrow. The count of corporate holders grew significantly. The actual demand depth — measured by free BTC, funding quality, and business incentives to hold — is concentrated in a handful of vehicles, and some of those vehicles are now operating in a more complicated environment than the one that drove their initial accumulation.

This matters because the next leg of the adoption story requires a different kind of entrant. Not another Strategic treasury company issuing converts to buy BTC, but traditional corporates and, eventually, institutional allocators treating Bitcoin as a genuine reserve asset.

## The Longer Arc Holds — but the Short Game Is Harder Than It Looks

The structural case for Bitcoin as an institutional reserve asset remains intact, and we think the corporate treasury trend is a genuine part of that story, not a distraction. It reduces dependence on retail sentiment cycles. It embeds BTC in institutional portfolio logic in ways that compound over time. And the M2 tailwind, when it actually materializes, will find a more institutionally mature market than any previous cycle.

But quality of positioning matters more than headline count right now. How it's funded, how encumbered the BTC is, what the underlying business looks like — these variables determine whether corporate adoption translates into sustained demand or just a more sophisticated form of the same concentration risk we've always had. The number of companies on the list is less important than what they're actually doing with what they hold, and why.
