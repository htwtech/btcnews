Bitcoin was never meant to host stablecoins. But over the past decade, different ideas have tried to make it happen. From Omni and Liquid to Lightning and now Plasma, new layers keep testing how the dollar can live on top of Bitcoin. Each approach reflects the same challenge: combining Bitcoin’s security with the everyday stability of USD.

## The first step: Omni Layer

In 2014, Tether launched its first tokens on the Bitcoin blockchain via the Omni Layer. It worked, but was slow and expensive. As Ethereum and Tron offered faster rails, Tether later announced it would stop supporting Omni. This early stage showed both the potential and the limits of stablecoins directly on Bitcoin.

To address these limits, developers turned to sidechains.

## Sidechains and federations

Liquid Network, launched by Blockstream in 2018, became the first major federated Bitcoin sidechain. It enabled fast, private transfers of Bitcoin and assets like USDT. Traders used it for exchange settlements, but it required trust in a federation.

Rootstock (RSK) took another approach: a merge-mined smart contract sidechain. Its protocol Money on Chain issued the Dollar on Chain (DoC) — the first Bitcoin-backed stablecoin. Unlike fiat-backed Tether, DoC is fully collateralized with BTC.

But not only sidechains explored stablecoins. Another path came with Stacks.

## Smart contracts tied to Bitcoin

Stacks introduced a separate blockchain tied to Bitcoin via Proof-of-Transfer. On it, projects like Arkadiko created the USDA stablecoin, letting users borrow against collateral. While not yet large-scale, Stacks showed that Bitcoin could support DeFi-style stablecoins.

If Stacks focused on smart contracts, Lightning targeted payments.

## Lightning and Taproot Assets

The Lightning Network brings instant payments to Bitcoin. With the Taproot Assets protocol, stablecoins can now be issued on Bitcoin and sent over Lightning channels. This could allow dollar-denominated micro-payments as fast as sending sats.

There are also creative models like Stablesats, which peg a balance to USD using derivatives without minting a new token.

While Lightning focuses on speed, Plasma sets its sights on scale.

## Plasma: new focus on stablecoins

The newest arrival is Plasma, a Bitcoin-anchored sidechain launched in 2025. It’s EVM-compatible and built mainly for stablecoins. The focus is on quick, almost free transfers, with direct support from Tether at launch. In Plasma, users can even cover fees in stablecoins or BTC instead of a native token. With backing from major investors, it aims to turn Bitcoin into a hub for stablecoin payments. This naturally raises the question of how stablecoins on Bitcoin differ by their design — whether they are centralized or decentralized.

## Centralized vs decentralized models

Stablecoins on Bitcoin can be grouped not only by technology, but also by their trust model. Centralized fiat-backed tokens like early USDT rely on issuers and custodians to maintain the peg. Decentralized crypto-backed tokens like DoC or USDA use BTC or other crypto as collateral, with rules enforced by smart contracts. There are also synthetic or derivative-based models like Stablesats that peg value without issuing a token. Plasma, though EVM-compatible, still focuses on integration with centralized issuers such as Tether, showing that both paths continue to evolve side by side.

## Why it matters

Every approach trades off decentralization, speed, and trust. Omni showed feasibility, Liquid proved speed, RSK enabled BTC-backed stablecoins, Stacks brought DeFi logic, Lightning opened instant transfers, and Plasma is now testing scale and usability. Together, they form the evolving stablecoin landscape on Bitcoin.

## Conclusion

Bitcoin may not have been built for stablecoins, but the demand for stable dollars is pushing innovation around it. With multiple sidechains, Lightning protocols, and new entrants like Plasma, Bitcoin is quietly becoming part of the stablecoin economy again. The future will likely combine both centralized and decentralized models — some relying on issuers like Tether, others on BTC-collateral or synthetic designs — giving users different ways to access stability on top of Bitcoin.
